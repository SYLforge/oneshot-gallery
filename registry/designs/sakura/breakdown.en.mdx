# SAKURA — breakdown

## The hook

There is a page that does only one thing, and it does it the whole way down:
black ink drops fall from a deep night sky, strike an invisible waterline,
and bloom into cherry petals that drift on a wind you can lean with your
hand and slowly fade. That is the entire premise — *ink & bloom* — and the
page refuses to decorate around it. No brand sells anything here; the page
is the product, performing the Japanese idea of *mono no aware* (ものの
あわれ), the gentle, appreciative sadness that beauty is transient. Every
petal is born, drifts, and is forgotten; the page stages the forgetting.

This is the Oneshot Gallery's labeled answer to
[oneshot-sakura](https://oneshot-sakura.vercel.app/) — the reference whose
ink-to-bloom premise inspired the gallery's `ink-bloom` family. The thesis
is the same premise, re-expressed from scratch: trilingual, installable,
fully source-included, MIT, and free. Where the reference offers one hero,
this ships the complete page, the tokens, and this honest prompt.

The design bet is the same one KEMURI (the washi-sumi-e flagship) made for
smoke, pointed the other way: a *simulated* bloom, advected through a real
fluid-physics curl-noise field, carries more feeling than any video of
falling petals — because a video ends, and this garden never does.

## Art direction

Ink-bloom lives or dies on the night. The ground is deep ink
(`#0e0a0f`), the speaking voice is a near-white warmed with the faintest
blush (`#f6eef0`, 17.2:1 — AAA), and there is one accent — blossom pink
`#e8869e` — which appears where a petal is alive: the kickers, the verse
numerals, the focus ring, the selection ground, the card frame. A single
warm mote, gold `#c9a85a`, appears exactly three places: the hero rule, the
seal stamp, the one drifting pollen grain. The moment a second warm hue or a
gradient blob arrives, the page becomes a screensaver.

One constraint did the hard work, and it is the opposite of KEMURI's.
KEMURI's ember failed AA on washi (2.9:1) and had to be banked; here the
blossom accent measures 7.8:1 on the ink-night ground — it clears AA even
for body text. So blossom is permitted to speak everywhere, and the palette
is unusually generous for a dark theme. `blossom-deep #c75a76` (4.6:1 on the
raised ink-panel) is reserved for the one place the full blossom would read
too bright. The page never inverts (KEMURI flipped to a dark room once;
here the dark is the home).

The type refuses the obvious trilingual trap. Shippori Mincho holds every
Japanese glyph — the vertical 桜花 title, the verse markers 一二三四, the
seal — because Shippori was drawn for vertical metrics first. Noto Serif KR
is the Korean main voice, the one that actually speaks (벚꽃, the verse
body, the ledes). Cormorant Garamond is the Latin subtitle — the wordmark,
the English beneath, the numerals. The stack order (`Cormorant, Noto Serif
KR, Shippori, serif`) means Hangul and kanji fall through the Latin face
into their own with no markup. The three languages are distinguished by
role, not just size: Korean speaks, Japanese decorates, English translates.

## Technique 1 — the ink-to-petal bloom (canvas-particles)

The naive way to animate falling petals is to give each a random wander and
a gravity tick. It reads as confetti, or snow — because independent random
walks have no *structure*, and real petals on spring air move together,
eddies forming and persisting in an incompressible flow. You can get all of
that without a fluid solver, and this page reuses the exact trick KEMURI
uses for smoke — pointed downward.

Take any smooth scalar field ψ (the *potential*) and define the velocity as
its rotated gradient:

```ts
// useBloomCanvas.ts — divergence-free flow: velocity = (∂ψ/∂y, −∂ψ/∂x)
const cx =
  (psi(x, y + CURL_EPS, t) - psi(x, y - CURL_EPS, t)) / (2 * CURL_EPS);
const cy =
  -(psi(x + CURL_EPS, y, t) - psi(x - CURL_EPS, y, t)) / (2 * CURL_EPS);
```

This is the 2D *curl* of ψ, and a rotated gradient is divergence-free by
construction: ∂vx/∂x + ∂vy/∂y = 0. Whatever noise you pour into ψ, the
resulting flow conserves area — petals advected by it swirl around the
potential's contour lines forever without ever compressing. That single
property is the difference between petals on wind and petals on confetti.

ψ is two octaves of seeded value noise (feature sizes ~170px and ~64px),
with the field itself drifting leftward and upward:

```ts
// useBloomCanvas.ts — the wind field travels with the petals
function psi(x: number, y: number, t: number): number {
  const xr = x + t * FIELD_DRIFT_X; // −10 px/s
  const yr = y + t * FIELD_DRIFT_Y; // −14 px/s
  return (
    valueNoise(xr * GUST_LARGE, yr * GUST_LARGE) - 0.5 +
    (valueNoise(xr * GUST_SMALL + 23.1, yr * (GUST_SMALL * 1.3) + 7.7) - 0.5) * 0.4
  );
}
```

Without that drift, petals swim through frozen turbulence and look like
they're climbing a rope; with it, each gust is born, travels, and dies with
the petals it belongs to. (KEMURI's smoke rises its field upward at 26 px/s;
this garden drifts its field left-and-up at (−10, −14), the way spring
weather actually moves.)

Where this page diverges from KEMURI is the *source*. Smoke is a continuous
plume from a censer mouth; petals here are *born* in events. An ink drop
falls under gravity (580 px/s², terminal 480 px/s); the moment it crosses
the waterline at `y = 0.42·H`, it splashes (4–7 micro-droplets flung
upward, life 0.5 s) and **blooms** — a ring of 8–14 petal particles spawns
outward at 40–90 px/s with an angular spread, then hands them to the curl
field:

```ts
// useBloomCanvas.ts — the bloom at the waterline
const bloom = (x: number) => {
  const n = BLOOM_MIN + ((rand() * BLOOM_SPREAD) | 0);
  const baseAngle = rand() * Math.PI * 2;
  for (let k = 0; k < n; k++) {
    // find a dead petal slot, recycle it into a fresh outward-bound petal
    const ang = baseAngle + (k / n) * Math.PI * 2 + (rand() - 0.5) * 0.8;
    const spd = BURST_VEL_MIN + rand() * BURST_VEL_SPREAD;
    parts[o] = x; parts[o + 1] = waterY;
    parts[o + 2] = Math.cos(ang) * spd;
    parts[o + 3] = Math.sin(ang) * spd * 0.6 + PETAL_SINK; // they sink, not rise
    // ...
  }
};
```

Petals sink at −22 px/s — cherry blossoms *fall*, they do not rise, which
is the inversion of KEMURI's +46 px/s smoke buoyancy. They relax toward the
field at 1.8 s⁻¹ (inertia, not obedience), live 7–13 s, and fade on a
square-root curve so they cross "barely there" early and spend most of
their life as a whisper. Three prerendered sprite tones (fresh blossom, mid
bloom, pale edge) give variety without per-particle shading; each petal is
one `drawImage` of a 5-pointed notched-blossom path. Before first paint the
system pre-advects 90 steps of 90ms, so you never see the garden being
planted — it was blooming before you arrived.

## Technique 2 — the wind that leans toward your hand (pointer-parallax)

Two outside influences bend the garden without ever steering it.

- **Pointer.** A capped horizontal pull (≤ ±70 px/s) toward the cursor,
  gated by an energy value with asymmetric lerps — attack 0.06, release
  0.02 per 60fps frame. The petals notice your hand in about a quarter
  second and take three to forget it. The pull's reach falls off with
  vertical distance from the pointer (a Gaussian over `H`), so a hand at
  the top of the garden barely stirs the petals at the bottom — wind has
  locality. On touch, or after 2.6 s of silence, the energy decays to zero
  and the autonomous breeze takes back over.
- **Click/tap.** A pointer-down pushes an `InkDropRequest` into a queue
  that the canvas's rAF loop drains: a fresh drop is released from the top
  of the stage above the tap's x, falls, and blooms where you touched. This
  is the signature interaction — you drop ink, and the garden answers. The
  queue is never coalesced, so a rapid double-tap yields two blooms.

```ts
// usePointerInk.ts — click/tap enqueues an ink drop, pointer-move sets wind bias
const onPointerDown = (ev: PointerEvent) => {
  const { x } = localXY(ev.clientX, ev.clientY);
  drops.current.push({ x, y: 0, t: performance.now() });
};
```

The whole pointer subsystem never re-renders React. `usePointerInk` writes
raw position + energy into a ref; the canvas's own rAF loop reads it and
frame-rate-normalizes the attack/release. This is the same pattern KEMURI's
`useScrollVelocity` uses for scroll, lifted to pointer space.

## Technique 3 — petals that arrive with your reading (scroll-scrub-pinned)

The verse section is the soul of the page, and it is the one place the bloom
is coupled to scroll. The section is ~260vh tall; its inner stage is
`position: sticky; top: 0; height: 100svh`, so the four verses *pin* in
place while you scroll through them. A `useScrollProgress` hook measures
the section's box against the viewport top into a ref (no React renders),
and two consumers read that ref:

1. **The bloom canvas** (via the `progress` prop). As `raw` runs 0→1, the
   autonomous drop interval shrinks (`AUTO_DROP_INTERVAL / (1 + p · 2.2)` —
   up to ~3.2× more drops at full progress) and a *density floor* enforces
   that at least `progress · 0.7 · count` petals are alive at any moment,
   recycling dead slots to meet it. So petals *accumulate* as you read the
   verses — the garden fills with bloom — and when you scrub back, the floor
   drops and petals clear naturally as they fade. Petals are not permanent;
   that is the entire point of mono no aware.

2. **The verse crossfade.** A tiny rAF loop lerps the raw progress into a
   smoothed value (frame-rate normalized at 8 s⁻¹) and writes
   `data-active="<i>"` onto the verse stack whenever the active index
   changes. CSS matches on it: the verse whose `data-i` equals the stack's
   `data-active` is opacity 1; the others fade and lift. The same change
   sets `aria-hidden` so a screen reader always reads the visible verse —
   scrubbing is not just visual, it is semantic.

```ts
// VerseScrub.tsx — smoothed progress drives both the canvas and the verse swap
smoothed.current += (raw - smoothed.current) * (1 - Math.exp(-dt * 8));
const active = verseForProgress(smoothed.current);
if (stack.getAttribute("data-active") !== String(active)) {
  stack.setAttribute("data-active", String(active));
  // ... and update aria-hidden on each verse to match
}
```

The four verses are the mono no aware sequence — *arrival → bloom → fall →
memory* — and scrubbing is how you walk through them. Scrub forward and the
garden fills; scrub back and it empties; the verses crossfade to match. The
pinned stage means you can dwell on any one as long as you like, and the
bloom keeps arriving underneath it.

## Motion math

Two numbers give the garden its temperament:

- **Velocity relaxation 1.8 s⁻¹.** Per frame, a petal covers
  `1 − e^(−dt·1.8)` of the gap between its velocity and the field's — a
  half-life of ~385ms. Heavier than KEMURI's smoke (2.4 s⁻¹, ~290ms) because
  petals have more drag than smoke; the garden drifts where the smoke
  climbed. Framerate-independent by construction, computed from `dt`.
- **Attack 0.06 / release 0.02.** The pointer-energy lerps, normalized to
  60fps. Half-life ≈ 12 frames in, ≈ 34 frames out. The garden turns toward
  a hand about as fast as KEMURI's smoke, and forgets slightly faster —
  petals are lighter than smoke, and the wind moves on.

Everything else obeys two easings: `ease-bloom`
(`cubic-bezier(0.22,1,0.36,1)`) front-loads travel and spends the tail
settling, so 900–1100ms reads as unfurling rather than slow; `ease-ink`
(`cubic-bezier(0.65,0,0.35,1)`) is symmetric and weighted for the two
physical moves — the loader's night lift and the button's bloom-fill.
Ambient cycles (waterline shimmer 6.2s, hint drift 8.6s) are mutually prime
with each other and with the breeze periods (~33s, ~15s), so the idle garden
never visibly loops.

## Performance notes

- One rAF loop for the bloom, throttled to ~64fps (`FRAME_MS = 15.5`),
  `dt` clamped to 48ms so a background tab never causes a lurch on return.
  The loop stops via IntersectionObserver (80px margin) *and*
  `visibilitychange` — zero frames offscreen or tab-hidden.
- DPR capped at 2; petal count scales with stage area (120–520). A petal
  costs 8 value-noise evaluations for the curl (4 ψ samples × 2 octaves)
  and one `drawImage` of a prerendered sprite — no per-particle
  `createRadialGradient`, no shadows, no per-frame path building.
- The warmup (90 × 90ms steps) runs once, synchronously, at mount: ~8s of
  simulated time for about a frame's worth of work.
- The verse binder is a separate featherweight rAF (also throttled,
  15.5ms) that does six lines of math and at most one DOM attribute write
  per change — it never re-renders React.
- All CSS animation is `transform`, `opacity`, `filter`. Reveals write
  `class` + `transitionDelay` from one observer callback; the verse swap
  writes one `data-active` attribute. No React re-renders on scroll
  anywhere on the page.

## Accessibility notes

- **Reduced motion:** the loader never mounts (and `display: none` as a
  belt), the bloom warms up and paints one composed still (the garden
  mid-bloom, one drop frozen mid-fall, petals resting where the breeze
  holds them), the verse section shows the first verse statically, and a
  media query flattens every transition and ambient cycle. The result is a
  finished garden, not a paused video.
- **No-JS:** every pre-reveal style is gated behind `.sakura-js`, added on
  mount; the SSR page is the completed page — the first verse visible, the
  wordmark present, the garden's meaning conveyed by the canvas's
  `role="img"` label even though the canvas itself is empty without JS.
- **Semantics:** every canvas stage is `role="img"` with a bilingual
  (KO/EN) description; the wordmark is an `h1` with `aria-label`; the
  vertical 桜花 is `aria-hidden` (decorative); the verse stack manages
  `aria-hidden` so only the active verse is read; decorative seals are
  `aria-hidden`.
- **Keyboard & touch:** the two interactive elements (the bloom-again
  button, the top link) take a 2px blossom ring on `:focus-visible`. The
  garden self-drifts on touch; pointer-down drops ink for everyone;
  nothing anywhere lives behind hover.
- **Contrast:** petal-white 17.2:1, bloom 12.6:1, blossom 7.8:1 (even
  text-safe), gold 8.6:1, ink-soft 7.3:1 — all on ink-night. Moss-ink
  (3.1:1) is large/decorative only and never carries meaning. Worst case
  under a passing petal, the wordmark still reads ≈ 9:1.

## How it answers oneshot-sakura

The reference site inspired the `ink-bloom` family and, in part, the
gallery. This entry is the labeled response: the same unprotectable premise
(ink falls and blooms into petals), re-expressed from scratch with no
borrowed code or assets, and shipped under MIT with the complete source, the
tokens, and this honest prompt. Where the reference offers one hero, SAKURA
ships a full page — hero, four scrubbed verses, an open garden, a footer —
trilingual (Korean main, Japanese decorative, English subtitle), reduced-
motion safe, no-JS degradable, and installable with one command. The
ink-to-petal transition is built on a real divergence-free curl-noise field
(the same engine KEMURI uses for smoke, inverted to sink), not a canned
animation; and the scroll-scrubbed verse section — where petals accumulate
as you read and clear as you leave — is a signature moment the reference
does not have.

## Remix ideas

1. **Autumn.** Keep the curl field and the bloom-on-impact event; swap the
   petal sprite for a maple leaf (a seven-pointed path), shift the palette
   from blossom to ember-and-rust, and let the "drops" become rain. The
   waterline becomes a puddle; mono no aware becomes *tsuyu* — the autumn
   dew.
2. **Snow garden.** Invert the bloom: particles spawn at the top (not the
   waterline), drift down with near-zero buoyancy and high drag, and never
   bloom — they *accumulate* at the bottom of the stage, building a static
   snowfield that the scroll-scrub clears. The verse becomes silence.
3. **Bonfire night.** Replace the ink drop with an ember and the petal with
   a spark; the bloom becomes a *burst* (radial, high velocity, short life,
   gravity returning them down). The curl field gains a buoyancy pulse on
   each burst. Palette shifts to a single warm gold on the ink-night — the
   garden becomes a fireplace.
